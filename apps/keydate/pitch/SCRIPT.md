# KeyDate — 3-minute pitch script

JCI Edmonton, Creative Young Entrepreneur. 3 minutes, then 1 minute of Q&A.

~440 spoken words. At a measured pace that lands around **2:50–2:55**, leaving
room to breathe. Don't speed up to add more — the pauses are doing work.

**One thing only you can supply:** slide 2 is marked `[YOUR DETAIL]`. Everything
else is written. That one line has to be true and specific, and it is the
difference between a room nodding politely and a room leaning in.

---

## Scoring map

The rubric is 115 points and badly uneven. This script spends its time where the
points are, not where the product is most fun to talk about.

| Criterion | Points | Carried by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business and international impact | **35** | Slide 7 |
| Community/social impact and sustainability | **20** | Slide 8 |
| Clarity of presentation | 15 | Delivery throughout |
| Innovation and originality | 15 | Slide 6 |
| Scalability and growth | 15 | Slide 9 |
| Leadership ability | 15 | Slide 9 + how you handle Q&A |

Slides 7 and 8 are 55 of 115 points between them. If you are running long, cut
from slide 4, never from those two.

## The script

### Slide 1 — KeyDate · 0:00

> Ask someone in their twenties when they'll own a home.
>
> They won't give you a year. They'll give you a feeling.
>
> *"Probably never."*

**Open cold. Don't read the slide.** Pause two full beats after "probably
never." That silence is the hook.

---

### Slide 2 — I bought my first place · 0:20

> I bought my first place. I got there.
>
> And I was guessing the entire way.
>
> **[YOUR DETAIL — one concrete thing, told properly]**
> *The month you nearly gave up. The person whose advice turned out to be
> wrong. The number you were short by.*
>
> Spreadsheets I rebuilt every month. Advice that contradicted itself. A target
> that moved every time I looked at it.

Say the detail like you're telling one person, not a room. This is the slide
that makes you a human being rather than a founder.

---

### Slide 3 — Nobody could tell me when · 0:50

> And the whole time, nobody could tell me *when*.
>
> How much do I actually need? Am I even close? Is this the year — or is it
> five years away?
>
> Every tool I found answered a different question than the one I was actually
> asking.

Say these like you're still annoyed about it. They were your questions.

---

### Slide 4 — One generation apart · 1:15

> In 2006 the average Canadian home was two hundred and seventy-seven thousand
> dollars. Today it's just under seven hundred thousand.
>
> Most of you bought somewhere inside that gap.
>
> If it was that hard for me — what is it for someone starting now?

**Don't linger.** This is evidence for a feeling, not the argument. If you're
running long, this is the slide to shorten.

---

### Slide 5 — So I built the thing I needed · 1:35

> So I built the thing I needed.
>
> Three questions — what you earn, what you've saved, what you can put away.
> Sixty seconds. *(hold up phone)* And you get back a real month.
>
> Then it builds the plan: FHSA, Home Buyers' Plan, the stress test — the
> Canadian rules nobody explains. And every dollar you save builds your house
> on screen.

**Have your plan already open on the phone.** Never type live, never trust venue
wifi.

---

### Slide 6 — We changed the question · 2:00

> Every other tool asks what you can afford *today*. Today the answer is no — so
> people close the tab and stop saving entirely.
>
> We ask *when*. And the answer is a date.
>
> **I can't make the house cheaper. I can give people the answer I never got.**

That last line pre-empts the sharpest question in the room. **Never cut it for
time.**

---

### Slide 7 — It was never just my story · 2:15 · 35 points

> When people can't see a path, they stop building wealth at all. Renters
> instead of owners. Wealth that never compounds.
>
> And Canada isn't special. Australia, the UK, Ireland, New Zealand — every one
> has a generation priced out and a tangle of government programs nobody
> understands.
>
> KeyDate is a rules engine with a Canadian rulebook loaded. The engine doesn't
> change at the border. **The rulebook does.**

Slow down on the last four words. Biggest-scoring slide in the deck.

---

### Slide 8 — The money we refuse to take · 2:35 · 20 points

> The obvious way to monetise this is to sell these users to mortgage brokers.
> People who just told you their income, their savings, and exactly when they'll
> need a mortgage. It's the most valuable lead list in the country.
>
> Our privacy policy says we never will. In writing, live today.
>
> Because the moment I'm paid to push you toward a house, I stop being able to
> tell you the truth. *(gesture at screen)* So we built the tool that tells you
> **not** to buy.
>
> **A company on referral fees can never say that sentence.**

Point at the screenshot when you say it. The app is on screen telling a user to
keep renting — let the judges read it.

---

### Slide 9 — Built, shipped, live · 2:50

> It's software. 467 Canadian municipalities today, near-zero cost per user, and
> a new country is a rulebook — not a rebuild.
>
> I built and shipped it myself. It's live at keydate.ca. It's incorporated.
> Privacy policy, terms, moderation — done before the first user, because I'd
> rather be trusted than fast.

Leadership is 15 points and you're solo. Lead with evidence, not adjectives.

---

### Slide 10 — Close · 3:00

> Owning a home isn't impossible.
>
> It just doesn't have a date yet.
>
> **We're giving it one.**

**Memorise this.** Never read the close. Then stop talking.

## Optional insert — traction

If signups come in before you present, add one sentence at the top of slide 9:

> I sent this to friends yesterday. [N] people joined in 24 hours.

Use the real number. If it's six, say six. A small honest number beats a vague
big claim, and this rubric has no line item for volume. When judges ask, say
what's true: "N people from a personal network, in 24 hours." Don't let it get
rounded up into "traction."

## Q&A — expect two questions

**"How do you make money?"**
Freemium. The planner stays free; the paid tier is couples planning together,
multiple properties, and deeper tax modelling. Deliberately not lead generation
— I'd rather have a smaller business I can be honest inside of.

**"What stops a bank from building this?"**
A bank can't build the tool that tells you to keep renting. Their incentive is
to originate mortgages. My entire product is credibility, and their business
model forbids it.

**"You're one person. What if you get hit by a bus?"**
Fair, and I won't pretend otherwise. What I can show is that I ship: this went
from idea to a live, incorporated product with legal infrastructure, solo. First
hire is an Alberta mortgage professional to validate the rules engine.

**"Where's the international proof?"**
None yet. Edmonton first, then Canada — I'd rather do one country properly than
four badly. The architecture separates the engine from the rules so expansion is
configuration, not a rewrite. That was deliberate.

**"Are those numbers right?"** (slide 4)
2006 is CREA's own figure for a record year — $276,974. Current national average
is just under $700,000. I deliberately didn't put an income figure on the slide,
because the comparable series doesn't line up cleanly against 2006 and I'd
rather use a comparison I can fully defend.

That last answer scores points by itself: it shows you know where your own data
is weak.
